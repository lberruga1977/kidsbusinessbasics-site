For parents and educators · Expenses

How to explain expenses to kids, with a lemonade stand

Kids see money come in all the time. A lemonade stand is a friendly way to show them where some of it goes before anyone gets to keep it.

November 15, 2026 · Goes with the lesson What Are Expenses?, for kids ages 7–12

The one idea: expenses are money out

Expenses are the money you spend to make or sell something. That's the whole idea, and it's enough for one conversation. A helpful partner word is revenue. Revenue is money in. Expenses are money out. Once your child can sort those two, a lot of everyday money talk starts to make sense.

There's one more piece worth naming early: not all money out is a business expense. If Leo buys a toy truck just for himself, that's personal spending, not a lemonade expense. Kids enjoy this distinction, because it turns sorting into a little game.

A short story: Maya and Leo's lemonade stand

Maya and Leo sold lemonade and made twenty dollars. Do they get to keep all twenty? Let's rewind. Before they sold a single cup, they went shopping for lemons, sugar and cups, and they paid with money from their startup wallet. That money went out. It was spent to make the lemonade, so it counts as expenses.

A quick check you can ask right away: is buying cups money in, or money out? Money out. Cups are an expense.

The real numbers, added together

Counting expenses is a gentle bit of addition, and children like to count along:

  • Lemons cost $5.
  • Sugar cost $2. Five plus two is seven.
  • Cups cost $1. Seven plus one is eight.

The lesson invites children to count along out loud: start with five, add two, that makes seven, add one, that makes eight. Saying each step helps a child see where the total comes from, instead of just hearing the answer.

Their expenses were $8. They took in $20, so with all their lemonade costs counted, twenty minus eight leaves $12. That last step is profit, which is the next lesson in the series, so you can simply mention it and move on.

The lesson then tries a second example. Leo sells his drawings and makes $15. Earlier, he spent $3 on paper and markers. His expenses were $3, because paper and markers were money out to make the drawings.

Questions to ask your child

Try one or two after you watch. Talk, write or draw. There's no score to aim for.

  1. Your sticker stand made eight dollars. You spent two dollars on stickers. What were your expenses? (Two dollars. The stickers were money out.)
  2. If Leo buys a toy truck just for himself, is that a lemonade expense? (No. That's personal spending.)
  3. Is buying cups money in, or money out?

A quick activity at home: let's sort

Near the end, the lesson has a sorting round you can repeat at the kitchen table. Say each item out loud and let your child call out the group:

  • Money a customer pays you: revenue.
  • Paper for drawings you'll sell: expense.
  • A snack just for you: personal spending.

Then swap roles and let your child quiz you. Try adding a few of your own everyday items, and when one is tricky, ask your child to explain their thinking before you give your view. There's no need for real money, a printer or any purchase. A pencil and scrap paper are plenty, and so is a quick chat in the car.

If your family ever decides to sell something for real, do it together, with a grown-up involved the whole way.

Recap

Expenses are money out, spent to make or sell something. Add them up carefully, and remember that not all the money that comes in is yours to keep. It's one idea in one short sitting. One idea. Five minutes.
