What is profit? A simple way to teach kids where money goes

When a child says “I made twenty dollars!”, it's a great moment to ask a follow-up question: how much do you get to keep? That's what profit is about.

November 15, 2026 · Goes with the lesson What Is Profit?, for kids ages 7–12

The one idea: profit is what's left

Profit is the money you get to keep after you pay your expenses. The lesson builds it in three steps that children can say out loud:

  1. Start with revenue, all the money that comes in when you sell something.
  2. Take away expenses, the money you spend to run your business.
  3. What is left over is profit.

Revenue minus expenses equals profit. If your child has already watched the Expenses lesson, this is the natural next step: one idea, built on the last one.

A short story: Maya's Saturday lemonade stand

Maya ran a lemonade stand on Saturday. Customers paid her $20. But she spent $8 on lemons, sugar and cups. So how much money does Maya actually get to keep?

Here's the common mix-up the lesson names. Leo once said he made $20 of profit, because he sold $20 of lemonade. But $20 was his revenue, the money in. Leo forgot to take away what he spent. Revenue is not the same as profit.

The real numbers, with a check

  • Maya's lemonade stand: twenty dollars minus eight dollars equals twelve. Check it: eight plus twelve makes twenty.
  • Leo's drawings: revenue of fifteen dollars, three dollars on paper and markers. Fifteen minus three equals twelve. Different revenue, different expenses, but the same profit. That's why you always need both numbers.
  • Maya walks dogs: she earns ten dollars and spends four on leashes and treats. Ten minus four equals six. Check it: four plus six makes ten.

Sometimes expenses are bigger than revenue. Imagine you spend $10 on cookie supplies but only sell $6 of cookies. You spent four dollars more than you earned. There is no profit, and that is called a loss. It's a useful, low-stakes way to show children why counting costs matters.

Questions to ask your child

Try one or two. Talk, write or draw. There's no score to aim for.

  1. Your sticker stand had eight dollars in revenue. You spent two dollars on stickers. What was your profit? (Eight minus two equals six dollars.)
  2. Look at the lemonade stand and the drawings. Which one spent more on expenses? (The lemonade stand: eight dollars, compared with three.)
  3. If you started a small business, what would your expenses be?

A quick activity at home: three questions

The lesson's review closes with a habit you can practice anywhere. Next time you pass a business, a bake sale or a market stall, wonder out loud together:

  • What came in?
  • What went out?
  • And what is left?

You don't need real figures, and you don't need to know a business's actual costs. Your child can guess what the costs might be, like cups, ingredients or paper, and talk through why revenue alone doesn't tell the whole story.

A good habit from the lesson is checking the answer by adding back. If the profit is twelve and the expenses were eight, does eight plus twelve make the twenty that came in? Children like having a way to prove they're right, and it turns subtraction into something they can test. Keep it light: talk, point or write it down, and stop whenever the conversation runs its course.

Recap

Revenue is the money in. Expenses are the money out. Profit is the money you keep. Start with revenue, take away expenses, and what is left is profit. Always take away the expenses first. One idea. Five minutes. The next lesson in the series is about giving every dollar a job: a budget.
