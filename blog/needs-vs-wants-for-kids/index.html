For parents and educators · Needs and wants

Needs vs. wants: a 5-minute conversation with your 7–12-year-old

“But I really need it!” Most families hear that sooner or later. This short conversation gives your child a clear way to tell a need from a want, without making wants feel bad.

November 15, 2026 · Goes with the lesson What Are Needs and Wants?, for kids ages 7–12

The one idea: needs come first

A need is something you must have to stay safe and healthy, like food, water, a warm coat or a place to live. A want is something nice to have, but you can live without it, like toys, games or treats.

The lesson is careful to say that wants are not bad. They're part of life. The point is simply the order: when money is limited, needs come first.

There's a common mix-up worth naming. Leo thought that if he wants something a lot, it must be a need. But wanting something a lot does not make it a need. A need is about staying safe and healthy, not about how much you want it.

A short story: Maya's class trip

Maya is going on a class trip with $10 for the day. She needs lunch, which costs $6. Then, at the gift shop, she sees a toy for $8. She can't buy both. Which one should come first?

This is a great place to pause and let your child answer before you go on. Most children get it quickly once they hear the definitions: lunch is the need, so it comes first.

The real numbers

  • Lunch plus the toy: six plus eight equals fourteen. That's more than ten, so Maya can't buy both.
  • Lunch first: ten minus six leaves four dollars.
  • Maya can save that $4 toward the toy for another day.

The lesson's second example uses school supplies. Leo has $12. He needs a notebook for $3 and pencils for $2, and he wants a toy robot for $10. Needs first: three plus two equals five, and twelve minus five leaves seven. Seven dollars isn't enough for the robot yet, so Leo can save for it and plan for it later.

Some things are a little of both. Shoes for school are a need, but fancy shoes with flashing lights are a want. The need is the shoes. The extra fancy part is the want.

Questions to ask your child

Pick one or two. Talk, write or draw. There's no score to aim for.

  1. Think back to Maya's class trip. What did she pay for first? (Lunch, because it's a need.)
  2. You have twelve dollars. Lunch costs five dollars, and a comic book costs nine dollars. Which do you pay for first, and how much is left? (Lunch first. Twelve minus five leaves seven dollars to save toward the comic book.)
  3. What is something you want, and how could you plan for it?

A quick activity at home: needs or wants?

The lesson sorts four things together: water, a winter coat, candy and a video game. Water and a winter coat are needs. Candy and a video game are wants.

Try the same game with things around your home. Name an item, and let your child decide which group it belongs to and explain why. When something is a little of both, like shoes, ask which part is the need and which part is the extra. Before any real purchase, your child can practice the lesson's three questions: Is it a need or a want? Are my needs paid for first? Can I save or plan for my want?

Recap

A need is something you must have to stay safe and healthy. A want is something nice to have. Wants are okay, but needs come first, and what's left can go toward planning for a want. One idea.
