How to teach kids to save money

A new year is a natural time to pick a first savings goal together. You can start in January, or any week that works for your family. Here is a simple way to teach kids to save money: choose a goal, save a little at a time, and watch it add up.

November 25, 2026 · Goes with the lesson What Is Saving?, for kids ages 7–12

The one idea: saving is a choice you make on purpose

Saving means keeping some of your money now, so you can use it later. A savings goal is the thing you are saving for, and how much it costs. When you save a little at a time, the amounts add up until you reach your goal.

The lesson names a common mix-up: saving does not just mean the money you forgot to spend. Saving is a choice you make on purpose, before the money is gone.

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau groups school-age children and preteens together, from ages 6–12, as a time when a habit of putting money aside can start to take root. In its research on how money skills grow, the Bureau also notes that during these middle-childhood years, children form money habits in part by watching and talking with the grown-ups around them.

The 2021 National Standards for Personal Financial Education, from the Jump$tart Coalition and the Council for Economic Education, say that by the end of fourth grade, students should know that when people save money, they are choosing not to spend it today so they can buy something in the future. A savings plan is a plan for setting money aside for a future need, goal, or emergency.

A short story: Leo's book, and Maya's three dollars a week

Leo's lemonade stand made $12 in profit. He really wants a book that costs $15. He doesn't have enough yet. Should he spend the twelve dollars on snacks today, or keep some for the book?

Keeping money for later is called saving.

Maya has a clear goal: a book that costs $15. She saves $3 every week. After one week she has $3. After two weeks, $6. After three weeks, $9. After four weeks, $12. After five weeks, $15 — exactly the price of the book.

Leo tries a different plan with the same $12 of profit. He decides to save $5 and spend $7 on a fun day with friends. Five plus seven equals twelve. He still had fun today, and he kept five dollars for later. You don't have to save every dollar. Saving some and spending some can both be part of a good plan.

The real numbers

  • Maya's book: $15. She saves $3 a week. Five weeks of three dollars is fifteen dollars.
  • Leo's profit: $12. He saves $5 and spends $7. Five plus seven equals twelve.
  • Leo's game: $20. He saves $4 a week. Counting by fours — 4, 8, 12, 16, 20 — he needs five weeks. Five times four is twenty.
  • A practice check: you save $2 every week. After four weeks, two plus two plus two plus two equals eight dollars.

How to teach kids to save money at ages 7–9

Keep the first steps small and concrete. The Bureau's Money as You Grow materials for school-age children, including a guide for ages 6–9, start with a specific savings goal — knowing exactly what the money is for.

  1. Pick one goal and write the price. A book, a small toy, or something else your child can picture. A nearby goal is easier to practice with than a far-off one.
  2. Choose a safe place to keep the money, with a grown-up looking after it: a jar, a piggy bank, or a labeled envelope. The lesson suggests keeping it in one safe place so it isn't spent by mistake.
  3. Save a little whenever money comes in. The Bureau suggests a habit of setting aside at least a dime of every dollar, which is 10 percent. Treat that as a starting idea, not a rule. Any amount that is saved on purpose counts.
  4. Make the goal visible. In a Money as You Grow activity for ages 8 and up, children draw what they are saving for, write the total at the top of a piggy-bank picture, and color a little more each time money goes in.
  5. Talk as you go. When you save a little yourself, think out loud so your child can follow along. Notice progress, not only the last dollar.

How to teach kids to save money at ages 10–12

Older kids can take more of the planning themselves, still with a grown-up nearby.

  1. Start with the lesson's three questions: What is my goal? How much does it cost? How much can I save each week? Then do the math together, the way Maya's five weeks of three dollars add up to fifteen.
  2. Notice that some goals are close and some take longer. A CFPB classroom guide for elementary ages explains that a book or a game might take only a few weeks, while other things take much longer to save for.
  3. Let your child keep the tracker and update the numbers. If a plan does not fit — maybe the goal costs more than the weekly amount can reach in a reasonable time — change the goal, the weekly amount, or the timeline. Changing the plan is a normal part of saving.
  4. If it fits your family, look at a federally insured savings account together, or visit a bank or credit union. The Bureau also offers an optional matching idea: you put in 25 cents for every dollar your child saves. Use a match only if it is comfortable for your household.
  5. Talk about the trade-off. Saving means choosing not to spend that money today. The lesson is careful to say that a treat can still be part of the plan.

A quick activity at home: a simple goal tracker

You only need paper and a pencil. This builds on the lesson's "draw the thing you are saving for and write its price," and on the Bureau's color-in tracker.

  • Write or draw the goal, and put the price at the top.
  • Choose a weekly amount you can really set aside. Draw equal boxes for each step — for Maya's book, five boxes of three dollars.
  • Each time money is saved, write the amount, color in a box, and add the new total.
  • Check the plan: do the boxes add up to the price? If not, change a number until they do.

If your child wants a second version, try a new weekly amount and see how the number of weeks changes. There is more than one good plan.

Questions to ask your child

Pick one or two. Talk, write or draw. There's no score to aim for.

  1. What is something you would like to save for, and how much does it cost?
  2. You save two dollars every week. How much have you saved after four weeks? (Two plus two plus two plus two equals eight dollars.)
  3. Maya's book costs fifteen dollars, and she saves three dollars a week. How many weeks does she need? (Five weeks. Five times three is fifteen.)

Recap

Saving means keeping some of your money now, so you can use it later. Pick a goal, save a little at a time, and watch it add up. Ask three questions: What is my goal? How much does it cost? How much can I save each week? A simple paper tracker makes the plan visible, whether you start in the new year or on an ordinary week. One idea. Five minutes.
