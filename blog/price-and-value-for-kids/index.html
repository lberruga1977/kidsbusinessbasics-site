For parents and educators · Price and value

Price vs. value: a 5-minute conversation with your 7–12-year-old

Maya sees two sticker packs at the store. One costs two dollars, and the other costs three. The cheaper one must be the better deal, right? Not always. This short conversation gives your child a clear way to tell price from value.

November 22, 2026 · Goes with the lesson What Are Price and Value?, for kids ages 7–12

The one idea: look at price and value

Price is how much money you pay for something. It is the number on the tag. Value is how much that thing is worth to you. It can mean how useful it is, how long it lasts, or how much you get.

Two kids can buy the same ball for the same price, five dollars. If one plays with it every day, it has lots of value to her. If the other never plays with it, it has little value to him.

There's a common mix-up worth naming. Leo thought the cheapest thing is always the best deal. But a low price does not always mean good value. And a higher price is not always better, either. Smart shoppers look at both the price and the value before they buy.

A short story: two sticker packs

Pack A has ten stickers for two dollars. Pack B has twenty stickers for three dollars.

This is a great place to pause and let your child think before you go on.

The real numbers

  • Pack A: two dollars split into ten stickers is twenty cents for each sticker.
  • Pack B: three dollars split into twenty stickers is fifteen cents for each sticker.
  • Pack B has the higher price, but each sticker costs less. If you will use all twenty, Pack B has more value for you.

But value is personal. If you only need ten stickers, the extra ten might go to waste. Then Pack A could be the better choice for you.

The lesson's second example uses water bottles. Bottle A costs four dollars and breaks after one month. Bottle B costs eight dollars and lasts for one year. If you kept buying Bottle A every month for a year, that is twelve times four dollars. That's forty-eight dollars. Bottle B costs only eight dollars for the whole year. So Bottle A is cheaper today, but Bottle B can be much better value.

The lesson also solves one together. Box A has eight crayons for four dollars. Box B has twenty-four crayons for six dollars. Four dollars split into eight crayons is fifty cents each. Six dollars split into twenty-four crayons is twenty-five cents each. Box B costs less for each crayon.

Questions to ask your child

Pick one or two. Talk, write or draw. There's no score to aim for.

  1. Think back to the sticker packs. Which sticker costs less, one from Pack A or one from Pack B? (Pack B. Each sticker costs fifteen cents, instead of twenty cents.)
  2. Two cookies cost four dollars. Five cookies cost five dollars. Which costs less for each cookie? (Five cookies for five dollars. Four dollars split into two cookies is two dollars each. Five dollars split into five cookies is one dollar each.)
  3. What is something you own that was really worth its price? Why?

A quick activity at home: price and value

Try the same idea with two similar things at home, or before the next small purchase. Look at the price, then ask the lesson's three questions: How much do I get? How long will it last? And will I really use it?

Recap

Price is what you pay. Value is what it is worth to you. Look at both the price and the value before you buy, and remember: the cheapest choice is not always the best value. One idea. Five minutes. Educational content for families, not financial advice.
