How we teach money in five minutes

Parents and educators often ask how our lessons are put together. Here is our approach, in a short version and a longer one, with an example you can watch.

November 15, 2026 · Goes with the lesson What Are Expenses?, for kids ages 7–12

Our approach in one sentence

Every lesson teaches one money idea in five minutes: a story first, everyday examples, a pause to think, and a quick recap, plus a free parent guide to practice at home.

Right now, our free parent guide goes with the Expenses lesson.

The longer version

Each lesson focuses on one idea, so kids aren't overloaded. It starts with a story kids can picture, like a lemonade stand or a sticker sale, then uses real numbers and everyday examples. Kids get a moment to think and answer before the reveal, and every lesson ends with a recap. A free parent guide lets families practice together at home.

Our catchphrase sums it up: One idea. Five minutes. It's a promise about focus, not a timer. Our lessons run about three to four and a half minutes, and any conversation afterward is up to you.

What that looks like in a lesson

Here's how each part shows up in the Expenses lesson, so you can see the approach in action.

  • One idea. Expenses are money out, spent to make or sell something. That's all the lesson asks a child to hold on to.
  • A story first. Maya and Leo sold lemonade and made twenty dollars. Do they get to keep all twenty?
  • Real numbers. Lemons $5, sugar $2, cups $1. Children count along: five, then seven, then eight dollars of expenses.
  • A pause to think. Quick checks like “Is buying cups money in, or money out?” give a moment to answer before the reveal.
  • A recap. Every lesson closes by restating the one idea in plain words.

The same pattern repeats across the series, so children learn what to expect: a question to wonder about, a few clear examples, a turn to answer, and a short review at the end. Most lessons also point to the next idea: needs and wants lead to price and value, and expenses lead to profit.

Maya and Leo are our visual hosts. One clear, warm adult narrator does the teaching, so children always know whose voice to follow.

Questions you can use with any lesson

The Profit lesson's review closes with three questions children can carry into everyday life. They work well after almost any of our money lessons:

  1. What came in?
  2. What went out?
  3. And what is left?

A quick activity: watch, talk, try

Our Expenses parent guide suggests a simple rhythm, and it works with any of our lessons. Watch together, and pause if something catches your child's interest. Talk: try asking, “Where did the money go?” and give your child time to explain in their own words. Try one prompt if you'd like. Talk, point or draw on scrap paper. Skip any prompt or stop whenever you like. No printer, purchase or real money is needed.

For classrooms and homeschool groups, the same rhythm works with a group: watch once together, pause at the quick checks so everyone can answer, then try one question out loud. Go at your group's pace.

Recap

Each lesson is built around one idea, a story kids can picture, real numbers, a moment to think and a recap. Our lessons are educational content for families and classrooms, not financial advice, and examples are kept simple on purpose. Start with the Needs and Wants lesson, then explore the rest of the series at your own pace.
