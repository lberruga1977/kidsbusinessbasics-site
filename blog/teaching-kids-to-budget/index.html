For parents and educators · Budgeting

Teaching kids to budget with real numbers

A budget sounds like a grown-up word, but the idea fits a child's ten dollars perfectly: make a plan before you spend, so every dollar has a job.

November 15, 2026 · Goes with the lesson What Is a Budget?, for kids ages 7–12

The one idea: every dollar gets a job

A budget is a plan for your money. It tells every dollar where to go, before you spend it. The lesson keeps the steps simple:

  1. Write down how much money you have.
  2. Decide how much goes to each part of your plan.
  3. Check that the parts add up to the money you have. Not more.

The order matters. The plan comes before the spending, not after, and the last step is always the check: add the parts and compare them with the money you started with.

Leo had a common mix-up: he thought a budget means you can never buy anything fun. But fun can be part of the plan. A budget just helps you choose on purpose, so you don't run out of money by surprise.

A short story: Maya's ten dollars

Maya has $10. She wants to save some, spend some on a treat, and share some with a friend. But if she spends it all first, there's nothing left for the rest. What if she made a plan before spending anything? That plan is a budget.

The real numbers

  • Maya's ten-dollar budget: save $5, spend $3, share $2. Five plus three plus two equals ten. Every dollar has a job.
  • Planning a class party: Leo's class has $12. Snacks $6, decorations $4, balloons $2. Six plus four plus two equals twelve, so the plan fits the money.
  • When a plan doesn't fit: what if the class wanted $10 of decorations? Six plus ten is already sixteen, which is more than twelve. They could spend $6 on decorations instead: six plus six is twelve. Changing the plan is a normal part of budgeting.
  • Leo's twenty dollars: save $10, spend $6, share $4. Ten plus six is sixteen, and sixteen plus four is twenty. Twenty equals twenty, so Leo's plan fits.

Questions to ask your child

Pick one or two. Talk, write or draw. There's no score to aim for.

  1. Look at the class party plan. Does it fit the twelve dollars? (Yes. Six plus four plus two is twelve.)
  2. You have fifteen dollars. You plan to save eight dollars and spend four dollars. How much is left to share? (Fifteen minus eight minus four equals three dollars.)
  3. If you had ten dollars, how much would you save, spend and share?

A quick activity at home: three boxes

This one comes straight from the lesson, and all you need is paper and a pencil.

  • Draw three boxes. Label them save, spend and share.
  • Pretend you have ten dollars, and write a number in each box.
  • Add your three numbers. Do they make exactly ten dollars?

If they do, the budget fits. If not, change a number until it does. Children often enjoy trying a second and third version, which is a nice way to show that there's more than one good plan.

When your child shares a plan, try asking about the choices rather than judging the numbers. Why did you put that much in save? What would you change if you wanted a bigger treat? The lesson's point is that changing the plan is normal, so a plan that doesn't fit the first time is part of the practice, not a mistake.

Recap

A budget is a plan for your money, and every dollar gets a job before you spend it. Ask three questions: How much money do I have? Where will each dollar go? Does it all add up? If the plan doesn't fit, change it. One idea. Five minutes.
